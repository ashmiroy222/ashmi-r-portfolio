Azure Monitor Health Models

Redesigning how people read system health inside Microsoft Azure, so someone under pressure can see what is failing, when it changed, and what to fix first.

Overview

Azure Health Models draws a cloud system as a tree of connected parts, each marked healthy, degraded, or unhealthy. People open it when something is already going wrong, and it was built for experts.

Our team of seven worked with two Microsoft engineers to make it clearer for both new and experienced users. I designed the Threshold Spectrum, a gauge that shows how close an entity is to failing. I also mapped and tested the Visibility of State designs.

Design question

How can we redesign Azure Monitor health models to enhance clarity and usability for both experienced and new users, without breaking what already works?

Who we designed for

New users

Interns and early practitioners who need guidance and quick value.

Experienced users

Engineers and architects who need speed, control, and depth.

Our persona, Joel, is new to Azure. He knows the concepts but not the interface, and has to decide what to fix first.

Background
What is Azure Health Models, and why is it hard to use?
Read moreRead less

Azure Health Models lets teams track the condition of the resources behind an application. It offers a graph, a timeline, custom health signals, and alerts sent to the people who manage the system.

Our scope was configuration, visualization, and alerting. Early on we learned there is no single experience to design for. What a user sees depends on how the system is set up and how familiar they are with Azure, so we shifted from streamlining one path to supporting both experts and newcomers.

Every idea also had to fit Microsoft's design system and be realistic for Azure's engineers to build.

My Contribution
My feature

Threshold Spectrum: how close is this to failing?

A degraded entity looks the same whether it just crossed the line or is minutes from failing. I designed a gauge that shows where an entity sits between healthy and unhealthy, so users can judge urgency at a glance.

How close am I to unhealthy from degraded? Question raised by an Azure user in our interviews
Sketch

The first idea was a gauge with unhealthy, degraded, and healthy zones and a needle for the current state. I also sketched a second arrow to show a previous state when a time filter is applied.

The goal was a visual aid that avoids dense numbers and timelines, driven by thresholds users already set.

Prototype

I placed the gauge inside the existing Entity Details screen, using Microsoft's own layout and components.

A value label sits under the needle, so state is never communicated by color alone. A Health Score card breaks the number down by signal, showing what is pulling an entity toward failure.

What testing changed

  • The gauge started as a smooth gradient. I switched to clear color blocks, which are simpler and make the number easier to read.
  • Later feedback confirmed it: users were unsure what "halfway to red" means on a gradient.

The open question

  • Each entity has its own signals with different units. One scale has to be built from the most impactful ones.
  • Thresholds would need to be set by users, and criticality also depends on context and rate of change.
  • The team's handoff lists feasibility research as the next step.
My work

Visibility of State: seeing the problem in a big tree

Health was shown mainly through color. One interviewee described 61 API and database calls just for a login page, which means 61 nodes to scan. Our team designed a way to focus on what matters. I mapped the current experience against the redesigned flow, then tested the designs with users.

Testing showed animation helps at scale and gives a second cue beyond color. A deselect option stops the animation, and unselected nodes were lightened so they read differently from unknown ones.

Team

Together these four features cut the time it takes to find and fix a state change.

Cursor states in the Designer

Pan and multi-select toggles on the canvas toolbar, so multi-select no longer depends on a hidden shortcut. Selected entities get a blue highlight.

Transition point for state

New State filters center the last state change on the timeline and flag the exact time it happened.

Health tooltip

Hover an entity to see which signals are degraded, such as 2 of 3, so users can prioritize faster.

Creating and editing entities

Radial meters count degraded and unhealthy signals inside each entity, and blue marks the selected one, matching Microsoft standards.

Outcome

What we worked within

  • One semester limited how many ideas we could design.
  • The platform is technical, and most of us had no technical background.
  • Designs had to fit Azure's design system and be feasible for engineers to build.

Handed off to Microsoft

  • Feasibility research on building each feature.
  • Testing at scale with much larger models.
  • More user testing with people from different backgrounds.

Design & Accessibility Approach

Prototyped in Figma on top of Microsoft's own interface, so every change looked and behaved like Azure. We checked designs against WCAG 2.1, including non-text contrast, focus visibility, labels, and animation control.
